I rise in firm opposition to the motion. The call for strict laws to regulate Large Language Models is a well-intentioned but fundamentally flawed proposition that would stifle innovation, cement corporate power, and create a false sense of security while failing to address the real challenges. My argument rests on three pillars: the impossibility of effective regulation, the superior alternative of adaptive governance, and the paramount importance of preserving open innovation.

First, the very nature of LLMs makes "strict laws" a practical impossibility and a regulatory quagmire. LLMs are not discrete products but evolving, general-purpose technologies whose capabilities and outputs emerge unpredictably from complex training processes. Legislating against specific outputs—like disinformation or biased text—is akin to passing a law against a printing press producing a harmful book. The liability would be impossibly diffuse, falling on developers, deployers, or users. Furthermore, the global and open-source nature of AI development means strict laws in one jurisdiction would simply push development underground or offshore, creating a dangerous black market while responsible actors are hamstrung by compliance. This would not stop bad actors; it would only disarm the good ones.

Second, the motion misdiagnoses the problem and proposes a cure worse than the disease. The proponent cites harms like disinformation and bias, but these are human societal problems that predate AI by centuries. Passing strict laws on LLMs is a distracting technical fix that lets our institutions off the hook for failing to promote media literacy, strengthen cybersecurity, and combat systemic discrimination. More critically, "strict laws" in a fast-moving field will inevitably be written by lobbyists for the largest tech incumbents, creating high compliance costs that only they can bear. This would erect a regulatory moat, crushing startups and academic research, and ensuring the future of AI is shaped solely by a handful of unaccountable corporations. This is the opposite of democratic accountability.

Third, there is a demonstrably better path: principles-based, adaptive governance focused on use cases, not the technology itself. Instead of preemptive, restrictive laws, we need agile standards, industry-led safety best practices, robust liability frameworks for clear harms, and significant investment in societal resilience. We already have laws against fraud, defamation, and discrimination; they must be applied to AI-generated content without creating a redundant, AI-specific bureaucracy. The focus should be on transparency and provenance—such as watermarking AI-generated content—empowering individuals to make informed choices, not on top-down control of a foundational technology.

In conclusion, strict laws are a premature, blunt, and counterproductive instrument. They would freeze innovation in its tracks, centralize power, and fail to achieve their stated safety goals. Our aim should not be to lock down this transformative technology with rigid statutes, but to guide its development with flexible norms, enforce existing laws on harmful applications, and strengthen society's capacity to harness its benefits. The risk is not in moving too slowly to regulate, but in rushing to regulate in a way that sacrifices our future prosperity and freedom on the altar of a perceived, but unattainable, safety. I urge you to reject this motion.
